Nayakkan Ramasamy 📜 The "Ancient Homeland" Myth Debunked: Historical, Legal, and Colonial Proof of the Disparate Origins of Sri Lanka's Tamil-Speaking Populations 🇱🇰

1️⃣ Pre-10th Century: The Inscription Evidence and the Word "Dameḍa" 🏛️
✍️ Early Sinhala Prakrit as the Standard: More than 1,200 Early Brahmi rock inscriptions found across Sri Lanka—including the North and the East—are written in Early Sinhala Prakrit. They show that an island-wide Buddhist monastery system and local society existed everywhere.📖 Source: Paranavitana, S. (1970). Inscriptions of Ceylon, Vol. I.

🏷️ "Dameḍa" as an External Label: The word Dameḍa (Tamil) appears only a few times in these early records. It was used as a special label for outsiders (for example, Dameḍa Vāṇija = "Tamil merchant") who came for trade or sea travel. There is no inscription evidence of an independent Tamil kingdom or government in the North or East during this time.📖 Source: Indrapala, K. (2005). The Evolution of an Ethnic Identity: The Tamils in Sri Lanka C. 300 BCE to C. 1200 CE.

2️⃣ Chola Period (993–1070 CE): Military Rule and Mercenary Soldiers ⚔️
🛡️ Invasion and Royal Service: Chola rulers attacked and destroyed Anuradhapura, ruling from Polonnaruwa for 77 years. After King Vijayabahu I defeated the Cholas and united the country in 1070 CE, South Indian soldiers stayed behind to serve in the Sinhalese king's army.

🪨 The Polonnaruwa Slab Inscription: The Velaikkara inscription in Polonnaruwa directly proves that South Indian mercenary troops were hired by the king to guard the Sacred Tooth Relic temple.📖 Sources: Nilakanta Sastri, K. A. (1955). The Cōḷas; Wickremasinghe, D. M. de Z. Epigraphia Zeylanica, Vol. II; Geiger, W. & Rickmers, C. M. (1929). Cūlavaṃsa, Part I.

3️⃣ Kalinga Magha’s Invasion (1215–1255 CE): Destruction and Regional Traditions 🔥
🏹 A Mercenary Army: The Cūlavaṃsa (Chapter 80, verse 61) states that Kalinga Magha invaded Sri Lanka with a 24,000-man mercenary army specifically described as "Keralas and Tamils" (Keralā damilā ceva). This invasion destroyed the ancient Rajarata civilization, forcing the Sinhala people and kings to move to the south and southwest.📖 Source: Cūlavaṃsa, Part II, trans. Geiger, W. & Rickmers, C. M. (1930).

🌴 Eastern Mukkuva Traditions: Local eastern texts like the Mattakkalappu Māṉmiyam state that Mukkuvar leaders were given land and local power (Vannimai) in the East during Magha's era. This matches the separate, mother-to-daughter inheritance system (Kudi) later documented in The Mukkuva Law.📖 Sources: Britto, C. (1876). The Mukkuva Law; Tambiah, H. W. (1950). The Laws and Customs of the Tamils of Jaffna.

4️⃣ The Aryacakravarti Rulers and Kotte Rule (13th–15th Centuries) 👑
🚩 Local Rulers under Pandyan Influence: The Aryacakravarti family took local control of the Jaffna peninsula with the help of the South Indian Pandya kingdom.📖 Source: Pathmanathan, S. (1978). The Kingdom of Jaffna: Part I.

🏰 Under the Kotte Kingdom: In the mid-15th century, Prince Sapumal, acting on the orders of King Parakramabahu VI of Kotte, captured Jaffna and governed it as part of the Kotte Kingdom.📖 Sources: Kokila Sandesaya; Selalihini Sandesaya; De Queyroz, Fernão. (1688/1930).

5️⃣ The Portuguese Era (1505–1658 CE): Direct Takeover and Coastal Allies ⛵
⚔️ End of Local Rule: In 1619, the Portuguese leader Filipe de Oliveira defeated King Cankili II, ended local dynastic rule, and placed Jaffna directly under the Portuguese Crown.📖 Source: Pieris, P. E. (1920). The Kingdom of Jafanapatam, 1645.

🌊 Coastal Groups: Coastal fishing and sea-trading groups—such as the Karaiyar and Catholic Paravar converts arriving from South India—built close ties with Portuguese trade and Catholic church networks.📖 Source: Pieris, P. E. (1913). Ceylon: The Portuguese Era.

6️⃣ The Dutch Era (1658–1796 CE): Rise of the Vellalar and the Thesawalamai 📜
🌾 Vellalar Land Power: The Dutch East India Company (VOC) gave government posts (Mudaliyar positions) and land privileges to the farming Vellalar caste to boost tobacco farming and tax profits, making them the top social group in Jaffna.📖 Sources: Arasaratnam, S. (1958). Dutch Power in Ceylon, 1658–1687; Pfaffenberger, B. (1982). Caste in Tamil Culture.
⚖️ The 1707 Thesawalamai Code: Dutch Governor Simons ordered Claas Isaaksz to write down the local customs of Jaffna, which were then checked and signed by 12 Vellalar Mudaliyars. This code legalized local property rules (which show strong Kerala Marumakkathayam roots) and formalized the master-servant caste system over the Koviar, Nallavar, and Pallar groups.📖 Sources: Memoir of Hendrick Becker (1716), trans. Sophia Anthonisz (1914); Tambiah, H. W. (1950).

7️⃣ The British Era: The "Malabar" Law and the 1911 Census Category 🇬🇧
🏛️ Legally Called "Malabars": British law did not call the community an "ancient Tamil nation." In Regulation No. 18 of 1806, the Thesawalamai was officially titled:"A Regulation for taking the Customs of the Malabar Inhabitants of the Province of Jaffna, usually called the Tesawalamai."
📊 The 1911 Census Split: In 1911, the British census officially separated people into two administrative groups: "Ceylon Tamils" and "Indian Tamils."📖 Source: Denham, E. B. (1912). Ceylon at the Census of 1911.

🧩 How the Modern Identity Was Built: The modern term "Ceylon Tamil" does not describe a single, uniform population that lived in an unbroken homeland for thousands of years. Different groups with totally different laws (such as Jaffna's Thesawalamai versus the East's mother-to-daughter Mukkuva Law) were grouped together in the late 19th and early 20th centuries through British census labels, a shared language, and politics led by Colombo-based elites.📖 Source: Russell, Jane. (1982). Communal Politics under the Donoughmore Constitution, 1931–1947.

ඉස්සර තිබුන සංරාජ පදවිය කියලා පදවියක්. ඔය වතු-ගිරි-පුර-රාම එන්න ඉස්සෙල්ලා. අන්න ඔය කාලෙ සංඝ අධිකරණය හරියටම ක්‍රියාත්මක වුණා. ඔය වතු-ගිරි-පුර-රාම නිකායන් සුද්දා යන්න ඉස්සෙල්ලා කරපු දෙයක්.

📜 The "Ancient Homeland" Myth Debunked: Historical, Legal, and Colonial Proof of the Disparate Origins of Sri Lanka's Tamil-Speaking Populations 🇱🇰

1️⃣ Pre-10th Century: The Inscription Evidence and the Word "Dameḍa" 🏛️
✍️ Early Sinhala Prakrit as the Standard: More than 1,200 Early Brahmi rock inscriptions found across Sri Lanka—including the North and the East—are written in Early Sinhala Prakrit. They show that an island-wide Buddhist monastery system and local society existed everywhere.📖 Source: Paranavitana, S. (1970). Inscriptions of Ceylon, Vol. I.

🏷️ "Dameḍa" as an External Label: The word Dameḍa (Tamil) appears only a few times in these early records. It was used as a special label for outsiders (for example, Dameḍa Vāṇija = "Tamil merchant") who came for trade or sea travel. There is no inscription evidence of an independent Tamil kingdom or government in the North or East during this time.📖 Source: Indrapala, K. (2005). The Evolution of an Ethnic Identity: The Tamils in Sri Lanka C. 300 BCE to C. 1200 CE.

2️⃣ Chola Period (993–1070 CE): Military Rule and Mercenary Soldiers ⚔️
🛡️ Invasion and Royal Service: Chola rulers attacked and destroyed Anuradhapura, ruling from Polonnaruwa for 77 years. After King Vijayabahu I defeated the Cholas and united the country in 1070 CE, South Indian soldiers stayed behind to serve in the Sinhalese king's army.

🪨 The Polonnaruwa Slab Inscription: The Velaikkara inscription in Polonnaruwa directly proves that South Indian mercenary troops were hired by the king to guard the Sacred Tooth Relic temple.📖 Sources: Nilakanta Sastri, K. A. (1955). The Cōḷas; Wickremasinghe, D. M. de Z. Epigraphia Zeylanica, Vol. II; Geiger, W. & Rickmers, C. M. (1929). Cūlavaṃsa, Part I.

3️⃣ Kalinga Magha’s Invasion (1215–1255 CE): Destruction and Regional Traditions 🔥
🏹 A Mercenary Army: The Cūlavaṃsa (Chapter 80, verse 61) states that Kalinga Magha invaded Sri Lanka with a 24,000-man mercenary army specifically described as "Keralas and Tamils" (Keralā damilā ceva). This invasion destroyed the ancient Rajarata civilization, forcing the Sinhala people and kings to move to the south and southwest.📖 Source: Cūlavaṃsa, Part II, trans. Geiger, W. & Rickmers, C. M. (1930).

🌴 Eastern Mukkuva Traditions: Local eastern texts like the Mattakkalappu Māṉmiyam state that Mukkuvar leaders were given land and local power (Vannimai) in the East during Magha's era. This matches the separate, mother-to-daughter inheritance system (Kudi) later documented in The Mukkuva Law.📖 Sources: Britto, C. (1876). The Mukkuva Law; Tambiah, H. W. (1950). The Laws and Customs of the Tamils of Jaffna.

4️⃣ The Aryacakravarti Rulers and Kotte Rule (13th–15th Centuries) 👑
🚩 Local Rulers under Pandyan Influence: The Aryacakravarti family took local control of the Jaffna peninsula with the help of the South Indian Pandya kingdom.📖 Source: Pathmanathan, S. (1978). The Kingdom of Jaffna: Part I.

🏰 Under the Kotte Kingdom: In the mid-15th century, Prince Sapumal, acting on the orders of King Parakramabahu VI of Kotte, captured Jaffna and governed it as part of the Kotte Kingdom.📖 Sources: Kokila Sandesaya; Selalihini Sandesaya; De Queyroz, Fernão. (1688/1930).

5️⃣ The Portuguese Era (1505–1658 CE): Direct Takeover and Coastal Allies ⛵
⚔️ End of Local Rule: In 1619, the Portuguese leader Filipe de Oliveira defeated King Cankili II, ended local dynastic rule, and placed Jaffna directly under the Portuguese Crown.📖 Source: Pieris, P. E. (1920). The Kingdom of Jafanapatam, 1645.

🌊 Coastal Groups: Coastal fishing and sea-trading groups—such as the Karaiyar and Catholic Paravar converts arriving from South India—built close ties with Portuguese trade and Catholic church networks.📖 Source: Pieris, P. E. (1913). Ceylon: The Portuguese Era.

6️⃣ The Dutch Era (1658–1796 CE): Rise of the Vellalar and the Thesawalamai 📜
🌾 Vellalar Land Power: The Dutch East India Company (VOC) gave government posts (Mudaliyar positions) and land privileges to the farming Vellalar caste to boost tobacco farming and tax profits, making them the top social group in Jaffna.📖 Sources: Arasaratnam, S. (1958). Dutch Power in Ceylon, 1658–1687; Pfaffenberger, B. (1982). Caste in Tamil Culture.
⚖️ The 1707 Thesawalamai Code: Dutch Governor Simons ordered Claas Isaaksz to write down the local customs of Jaffna, which were then checked and signed by 12 Vellalar Mudaliyars. This code legalized local property rules (which show strong Kerala Marumakkathayam roots) and formalized the master-servant caste system over the Koviar, Nallavar, and Pallar groups.📖 Sources: Memoir of Hendrick Becker (1716), trans. Sophia Anthonisz (1914); Tambiah, H. W. (1950).

7️⃣ The British Era: The "Malabar" Law and the 1911 Census Category 🇬🇧
🏛️ Legally Called "Malabars": British law did not call the community an "ancient Tamil nation." In Regulation No. 18 of 1806, the Thesawalamai was officially titled:"A Regulation for taking the Customs of the Malabar Inhabitants of the Province of Jaffna, usually called the Tesawalamai."
📊 The 1911 Census Split: In 1911, the British census officially separated people into two administrative groups: "Ceylon Tamils" and "Indian Tamils."📖 Source: Denham, E. B. (1912). Ceylon at the Census of 1911.

🧩 How the Modern Identity Was Built: The modern term "Ceylon Tamil" does not describe a single, uniform population that lived in an unbroken homeland for thousands of years. Different groups with totally different laws (such as Jaffna's Thesawalamai versus the East's mother-to-daughter Mukkuva Law) were grouped together in the late 19th and early 20th centuries through British census labels, a shared language, and politics led by Colombo-based elites.📖 Source: Russell, Jane. (1982). Communal Politics under the Donoughmore Constitution, 1931–1947.
