Should Gnanasara be released?
Perhaps the more important question is not who is asking for his release, but why a presidential pardon should be granted.
A President has the constitutional power to grant a pardon. But that power should never become a privilege exercised because of political influence, religious pressure, public emotion, or the identity of the person seeking it.
If a pardon is considered, the same principles should apply to everyone—
whether the person is a Buddhist monk, a politician, a powerful individual, or an ordinary citizen.
The Supreme Court has already found that the previous pardon was invalid. Therefore, any new decision should be made through a transparent and lawful process, carefully considering the circumstances of the case, the interests of justice, and any genuine grounds for clemency.
We should also be careful about another dangerous idea:
Respecting a religion does not mean giving anyone immunity from the law.
And seeking justice does not mean denying someone the possibility of mercy.
Justice and mercy can coexist—but mercy should never become a substitute for justice.
Perhaps this is bigger than one monk or one President.
It is about what kind of country we want to build:
A country where the law is applied according to the identity of the person—or a country where the same principles apply to everyone?
That is the conversation worth having.
